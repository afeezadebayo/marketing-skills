# Product-Led Growth Strategy

Product-led growth works brilliantly for some products and badly for others. It suits products individuals can try and benefit from quickly, at a price they or a small team can approve, where usage naturally spreads to colleagues. It struggles where value needs months of implementation, where buyers never touch the product or where trust and compliance demand human onboarding. Many companies choose PLG because successful peers did, rather than because their product and market fit it, and then bolt on sales when self-serve stalls.

This skill decides whether and how to be product-led: assessing fit, choosing a motion (pure self-serve, product-led sales hybrid, sales-led with a free entry point), defining the value moment and the path from free user to paying team, setting the operating model across product, marketing, sales and success, and choosing metrics.

## Operating principles
1. **Fit before fashion.** Choose PLG because users can reach value on their own, not because it's popular.
2. **The product is the first salesperson.** Design the experience so value is obvious without a demo.
3. **Hybrid is normal.** Most growing PLG companies add sales for larger accounts; plan for it.
4. **One value moment, clearly defined.** Everyone should know what activation means.
5. **Shared ownership.** PLG fails when product and go-to-market teams optimise different numbers.

## Motion options
| Motion | How it works | Fits when |
|---|---|---|
| Pure self-serve | Users sign up, activate and pay without talking to anyone | Low price, simple setup, individual or small team buyers |
| Product-led sales | Free or trial usage creates signals; sales helps larger accounts upgrade and expand | Individual adoption inside companies that buy at team or company level |
| Sales-led with free entry | Free tool, trial or assessment generates qualified conversations | Complex products with clear early value moments |
| Community-led with product | Communities and templates drive discovery; product converts | Practitioner tools with strong communities |

## Fit assessment
| Question | Strong PLG fit | Weak PLG fit |
|---|---|---|
| Time to first value | Minutes to days | Weeks to months |
| Who chooses the tool | End users or team leads | Procurement or committees only |
| Entry price | Individuals or teams can approve | Large contracts only |
| Setup | Self-serve with templates | Heavy integration or migration |
| Spread | Use naturally involves colleagues | Isolated single-user use |
| Market size | Many potential users | Few, large buyers |
| Compliance | Light | Heavy verification or contracts upfront |

## When to use, and when not to
Use it when launching a new product, considering a free plan or trial, moving from sales-led to product-led or adding sales to a self-serve product.

Don't use it to design the free plan details (`free-model-design`) or detailed pricing (the Product Marketing `pricing-packaging` skill).

## Inputs to ask for, in one message
1. Product, users, buyers and typical deal sizes.
2. Current motion and its results.
3. Time to first value and setup effort today.
4. Competitors' motions.
5. Team structure across product, marketing, sales and success.
6. Business goals and constraints.

## Workflow

### Step 1. Assess fit
Score the fit questions with evidence from users and data.

### Step 2. Choose the motion
Pick the primary motion and plan when sales assist joins (for example accounts above a seat count or spend).

### Step 3. Define the value moment
The action or result that shows a user has experienced value; validate it against retention data.

### Step 4. Map the path to revenue
Free or trial user, activated user, habitual user, team, paying team, expanding account.

### Step 5. Design the operating model
Who owns each stage; shared metrics; how product, growth, marketing, sales and success work together.

### Step 6. Plan the first improvements
Usually reducing time to value, clarifying the free model and adding product-qualified lead signals.

### Step 7. Set metrics and reviews
Activation rate, time to value, free-to-paid conversion, expansion, net revenue retention and payback.

## Path to revenue template
```
| Stage | Definition | Owner | Key lever | Metric |
| Visitor to sign-up | Creates account | Marketing | Clear value, low friction | Sign-up rate |
| Sign-up to activated | Reaches value moment within 7 days | Product and growth | Onboarding, templates | Activation rate |
| Activated to habit | Uses weekly for 4 weeks | Product | Core value, reminders | Week-4 retention |
| Habit to team | Invites colleagues | Product | Collaboration features | Invite rate |
| Team to paid | Upgrades | Product, sales assist | Limits, prompts, PQLs | Free-to-paid rate |
| Paid to expansion | Adds seats or plans | Success, sales | Usage growth | Net revenue retention |
```

## Operating model
| Team | PLG responsibility |
|---|---|
| Product and growth | Activation, onboarding, in-product upgrades, experiments |
| Marketing | Acquisition, positioning, education, community |
| Sales | Product-led sales on qualified accounts |
| Customer success | Expansion, retention, enterprise onboarding |
| Data | Tracking, PQL models, dashboards |
A weekly PLG review across these teams keeps the motion connected.

## Validating the value moment
Compare retention for users who completed each candidate action in their first week against those who didn't. Pick the action that best separates retained from churned users, is reached by a meaningful share of sign-ups and can be influenced by product changes. Recheck it every six to twelve months as the product changes.

## Edge cases and judgement calls
| Situation | What to do |
|---|---|
| Enterprise product with individual users | Product-led sales; free usage for individuals, sales for company rollouts |
| Long time to value | Templates, sample data, guided setup, or a free assessment instead of full self-serve |
| Existing sales team resists PLG | Show how PQLs bring warmer leads; agree rules of engagement |
| Regulated industry | Gated trials with verification; human onboarding for regulated steps |
| Marketplace or two-sided products | Define value moments for each side |

## Worked example
A data visualisation startup sold through demos with long cycles. Fit assessment showed analysts could connect a spreadsheet and build a useful chart in minutes, individual analysts chose tools and team plans fitted departmental budgets. The company launched a free plan for individuals with a product-led sales layer for teams of ten or more, defined the value moment as publishing a first dashboard within three days and set up a weekly PLG review across product, marketing and sales. Within a year, most new revenue came from self-serve teams, and sales closed larger deals faster because prospects were already users.

## Common mistakes
- Choosing PLG because competitors did.
- No clear value moment.
- Ignoring when sales should help.
- Product and sales chasing different goals.
- Measuring sign-ups as success.

## Questions to ask before you decide
- Can a new user get real value without help, and how fast?
- Who chooses and who pays for the product?
- At what account size does sales add value?

## Self-check before you answer
- Is fit assessed with evidence?
- Is the motion chosen with a plan for sales assist?
- Is the value moment validated against retention?
- Are ownership and metrics shared across teams?
