# Newsletter Strategy

A newsletter is one of the few channels a company owns outright. Search rankings shift, social reach falls and ad costs rise, but a list of people who chose to hear from you stays. The best newsletters become something readers look forward to: a clear promise, a consistent format, useful or entertaining content and a voice. Most company newsletters are something else: a monthly roundup of blog links and product updates that readers ignore.

This skill plans and improves newsletters: positioning and promise, audience, format and cadence, content mix, growth, deliverability basics, monetisation or business goals, and measurement. Lifecycle and automated emails sit in the CRM and Lifecycle skills.

## Operating principles
1. **A clear promise.** Readers should know what they'll get and why it's worth their attention.
2. **Reader value first.** Useful insight, curation or entertainment, with company promotion in proportion.
3. **Consistency builds habit.** Same day, same format, same voice.
4. **Grow with permission.** Clear opt-ins and honest sign-up promises.
5. **Measure engagement that matters.** Clicks, replies, retention and business outcomes over vanity opens.

## Newsletter types
| Type | Example | Fits |
|---|---|---|
| Curated roundup with commentary | Five links that matter this week, with our take | Busy professionals in a fast-moving field |
| Single essay | One deep idea per issue | Founders, thought leaders |
| How-to or tactical | One technique with steps and examples | Practitioners |
| Data or research digest | Numbers and trends with analysis | Analysts, decision-makers |
| Product and customer news | Releases, tips, customer stories | Existing customers (often better as lifecycle email) |
| Community | Member highlights, events, discussions | Communities and memberships |

## When to use, and when not to
Use it when launching a newsletter, when open and click rates fall, when list growth stalls, when the newsletter has no clear purpose and when building an owned audience alongside search and social.

Don't use it for automated onboarding, retention and win-back sequences (the CRM and Lifecycle skills) or for deliverability problems in depth (the CRM `email-deliverability` skill).

## Inputs to ask for, in one message
1. Business goals for the newsletter.
2. Audience and what they care about.
3. Current list size, growth sources and engagement data.
4. Past issues and best performers.
5. Email platform and sign-up points.
6. Team capacity and subject matter experts.

## Workflow

### Step 1. Define the promise
One sentence: who it's for, what they get, how often. For example: "Every Tuesday, one practical idea to close your books faster, in five minutes."

### Step 2. Choose type, format and cadence
Match to audience and team capacity; consistency matters more than frequency.

### Step 3. Set the content mix
For example: 80% reader value (insight, how-tos, curation), 20% company (product, events, offers).

### Step 4. Design the template
Recognisable header, short intro, main content, one primary call to action, footer with preferences.

### Step 5. Plan growth
Sign-up forms on high-intent pages, content upgrades, social and partner promotion, referrals, events; always with clear consent.

### Step 6. Protect deliverability
Authentication, list hygiene, easy unsubscribe, consistent sending.

### Step 7. Measure and improve
Clicks, replies, unsubscribes, list retention and business outcomes; review monthly.

## Issue structure (example)
```
Subject: one specific promise (avoid clickbait)
Preview text: extends the subject
Intro: two or three sentences, personal and direct
Main piece: one idea, with an example or data
Quick hits: two or three short links with commentary
From us: one product tip or event (optional)
Sign-off: name of a real person; invite replies
```

## Growth tactics
| Tactic | Notes |
|---|---|
| Sign-up on high-intent pages | Blog posts, guides, tools, footer |
| Content upgrades | Templates or checklists tied to the article |
| Social previews | Share one insight from each issue |
| Partner swaps | Recommend each other's newsletters |
| Referral programme | Rewards for sharing, with fraud checks |
| Events and webinars | Opt-in at registration |
| Archive pages | Public issues that rank and attract sign-ups |

## Metrics that matter
| Metric | Why |
|---|---|
| Click rate | Interest in content (opens are unreliable due to privacy features) |
| Replies | Strongest sign of connection |
| Unsubscribe rate per issue | Content or frequency problems |
| List growth by source | Which sign-up points work |
| 90-day subscriber retention | Whether readers stay |
| Business outcomes | Trials, demos, purchases or pipeline from newsletter readers |

## Subject line guidelines
- Make a specific promise the issue keeps.
- Use the reader's words for the problem.
- Keep it short enough to read on a phone.
- Avoid clickbait and false urgency; it erodes trust and future opens.
- Test angles over several issues rather than judging one send.

## Sign-up promise
Tell people exactly what they will receive and how often: "One practical idea every Tuesday. Five minutes to read. Unsubscribe anytime." Then keep the promise. A gap between the sign-up promise and the issues drives unsubscribes and spam complaints.

## Welcome email
Send a welcome immediately with the promise, the best past issues, a request to reply with what the reader wants to learn and instructions to add the address to contacts.

## Archive and repurposing
Publish past issues on a public archive page, and turn strong issues into blog posts, social threads and talks. Good issues earn search traffic and new subscribers long after sending.

## List hygiene
Remove hard bounces immediately, send a re-engagement email to subscribers who haven't clicked in six months and remove those who still don't engage. A smaller engaged list delivers better than a large inactive one, and mailbox providers reward engagement.

## Cadence choice
Weekly builds habit fastest; fortnightly suits small teams; monthly works for deep essays or data digests. Pick the rhythm you can keep for a year.

## Edge cases and judgement calls
| Situation | What to do |
|---|---|
| Small list | Focus on quality and replies; growth follows good issues |
| B2B with customers and prospects | Separate newsletters or segments by need |
| Falling opens due to privacy features | Judge on clicks, replies and retention |
| No time for weekly issues | Fortnightly or monthly, but consistent |
| Sponsors or ads | Disclose clearly; keep reader value first |

## Worked example
A project management company sent a monthly newsletter of blog links and product updates; clicks were low and unsubscribes high. The relaunch promised "one practical idea for running client projects, every Tuesday", written by the head of customer success with real examples, plus two curated links and an optional product tip. Sign-up forms moved onto top guides with a template download. Within six months, click rates and replies rose sharply, the list grew steadily, and sales began citing the newsletter in deals.

## Common mistakes
- No clear promise.
- Company news instead of reader value.
- Inconsistent sending.
- Buying or scraping lists.
- Judging success on opens alone.

## Questions to ask before you plan
- Why would someone look forward to this email?
- What can we deliver consistently with our team?
- How does the newsletter support business goals?

## Self-check before you answer
- Is the promise clear and specific?
- Is reader value the majority of each issue?
- Is growth permission-based?
- Are metrics beyond opens tracked?
