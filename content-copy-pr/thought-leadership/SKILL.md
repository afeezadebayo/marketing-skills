# Thought Leadership

Thought leadership is content that changes how an audience thinks about a problem: an original point of view, backed by evidence and experience, from people with credibility to say it. It builds trust before buyers are ready to buy, earns media coverage and invitations, and increasingly shapes what AI answer engines say about a category. Most so-called thought leadership is neither: generic trend pieces, ghostwritten posts without opinions or rewritten versions of what everyone else says.

This skill builds thought leadership programmes for companies and executives: finding defensible points of view, gathering evidence, choosing voices, formats and channels, ghostwriting in the expert's voice, and measuring influence.

## Operating principles
1. **Say something.** A point of view people could disagree with is more valuable than a safe summary.
2. **Earn the right.** Base ideas on data, experience, customers or research only you have.
3. **Real voices.** Content must reflect what the named expert actually thinks, in their words.
4. **Consistency over virality.** Repeated, coherent ideas build reputation over months.
5. **Useful, not self-promotional.** Readers should learn something even if they never buy.

## Sources of original insight
| Source | Example |
|---|---|
| Proprietary data | Aggregated, anonymised product usage trends |
| Customer patterns | What the best customers do differently |
| Expert experience | Lessons from building, selling or failing |
| Original research | Surveys and studies |
| Contrarian analysis | Where common advice is wrong, with evidence |
| Frameworks | A named model that simplifies a messy problem |

## When to use, and when not to
Use it to build executive or company reputation, support a category or positioning, earn speaking and media opportunities, influence long B2B buying cycles and shape AI answers about your space.

Don't use it for product announcements (`digital-pr`, Product Marketing launches) or search-led how-to content (the Content SEO skills).

## Inputs to ask for, in one message
1. Business goals and audiences.
2. Experts available (executives, product, research, customer-facing leaders).
3. Positioning and the beliefs behind it.
4. Data, research or customer insight that can be shared.
5. Channels: LinkedIn, company blog, newsletter, podcasts, events, media.
6. Approval constraints (legal, communications).

## Workflow

### Step 1. Find points of view
Interview experts: what do they believe that the market gets wrong? What have they seen that others haven't?

### Step 2. Test defensibility
Each point of view needs evidence and a willingness to defend it publicly.

### Step 3. Choose voices
Who has credibility on each theme: founder, product leader, researcher, customer leader.

### Step 4. Choose formats and channels
Essays, LinkedIn posts, newsletters, podcasts, talks, bylined articles, reports.

### Step 5. Build a capture habit
Monthly recorded interviews with experts; ghostwriters draft from transcripts.

### Step 6. Write in the expert's voice
Use their phrases, stories and opinions; they review and approve.

### Step 7. Distribute and engage
Experts respond to comments, join discussions and repeat core ideas across channels.

### Step 8. Measure influence
Engagement from target audiences, inbound invitations, media mentions, sales conversations citing content, AI answer mentions.

## Point of view template
```
Belief: <what we think>
What most people think instead: <common view>
Why we believe it: <data, experience, examples>
What it means for the reader: <implications, actions>
What would change our mind: <honest limits>
```

## Expert interview questions
- What do you believe about this problem that most people get wrong?
- What's a mistake you see customers make again and again?
- What did you learn the hard way?
- What will be different in this field in three years, and why?
- Tell me about a specific customer or project that changed your thinking.

## Formats compared
| Format | Strength | Effort |
|---|---|---|
| Long-form essay | Depth and nuance; referenced for years | High |
| LinkedIn posts | Reach among professional audiences | Low to medium |
| Newsletter | Repeat attention from an owned audience | Medium |
| Podcast appearances | Trust through conversation | Low per episode |
| Conference talks | Credibility and networking | High |
| Bylined articles in trade media | Third-party authority | Medium |
| Research reports | Evidence others cite | High |

## Ghostwriting workflow
1. Record a 30-minute interview on one theme.
2. Transcribe and pull the expert's own phrases, stories and opinions.
3. Draft in their voice, keeping their examples.
4. Send for review with specific questions, not a blank request for comments.
5. Publish under their name only after approval.
6. Keep a voice profile (phrases, tone, topics they avoid) for future drafts.

## Influence metrics
| Metric | Source |
|---|---|
| Engagement from target accounts and roles | Social analytics, CRM matching |
| Inbound invitations (podcasts, talks, media) | Communications log |
| Media mentions and quotes | Media monitoring |
| Sales conversations citing content | CRM notes, sales feedback |
| Branded and expert-name search growth | Search Console, Google Trends |
| Mentions in AI answers for category questions | AI visibility tracking |

## Building a bench of voices
Start with the founder or one credible executive, then add product, research and customer leaders. Several voices reduce dependence on one person and let the company cover more themes with authority.

## Publishing rhythm
A sustainable rhythm for one executive might be two LinkedIn posts a week, one essay or newsletter issue a month, one podcast or event a quarter and one research-backed piece a year. Each format repeats and develops the same core ideas, which is how a reputation forms. Review themes quarterly and retire ideas that no longer hold.

Experts should reply to thoughtful comments within a day; conversation builds more reputation than broadcasting.

Keep a running list of ideas from sales calls and customer meetings.

## Edge cases and judgement calls
| Situation | What to do |
|---|---|
| Expert has no time | 30-minute monthly interviews; ghostwriter drafts |
| Legal caution | Agree topics and boundaries in advance; keep opinions on industry, not clients |
| Founder voice dominates | Add other experts over time to reduce key-person risk |
| Opinions without evidence | Gather data or examples before publishing |
| Controversial topics | Decide deliberately; be prepared to engage respectfully |

## Worked example
A cybersecurity company's executive posts were generic trend summaries with little engagement. Interviews with the CTO surfaced a strong view: most breaches at mid-sized firms come from misconfigured cloud permissions, not sophisticated attacks, backed by anonymised data from customer assessments. The programme published a short essay series, a data-backed report and LinkedIn posts in the CTO's voice from monthly interviews. Within a year the CTO was invited to speak at two major conferences, trade media quoted the data, and sales reported prospects referencing the essays.

## Common mistakes
- Generic trend content with no opinion.
- Ghostwriting that doesn't reflect the expert's views.
- Opinions without evidence.
- Inconsistent publishing.
- Measuring only likes.

## Questions to ask before you plan
- What do your experts believe that others don't say?
- What evidence do you have that competitors don't?
- Who will commit time every month?

## Self-check before you answer
- Does each piece have a clear, defensible point of view?
- Is it based on evidence or experience?
- Does it sound like the named expert?
- Is influence measured beyond likes?
