# Social Proof and Trust Signals

Visitors look for reasons to believe a claim and reasons to feel safe acting on it. Reviews, ratings, customer logos, case studies, usage numbers, guarantees, security badges, clear policies and real contact details all reduce perceived risk. Placed well, near claims and decisions, they lift conversion; placed badly, buried at the bottom or faked, they do nothing or damage trust. Regulators in many markets also act against fake or misleading reviews, so authenticity is a legal matter as well as a moral one.

This skill audits and improves social proof and trust across key pages: which proof to use, where to place it, how to collect more, how to present it credibly and how to test its effect.

## Operating principles
1. **Relevance over volume.** Proof from customers like the visitor beats impressive but unrelated logos.
2. **Place proof at points of doubt.** Next to claims, prices, forms and payment.
3. **Specific beats vague.** "Cut month-end close from 6 days to 2" beats "Great product!"
4. **Authentic only.** No fake reviews, invented numbers or misleading badges.
5. **Trust is also hygiene.** Clear policies, contact details and secure checkout matter as much as testimonials.

## Types of proof and trust
| Type | Best placement | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Star ratings and review counts | Product pages, near prices and add-to-cart | Show count, not just average |
| Written reviews | Product and service pages | Filters by use case or size help buyers find relevant ones |
| Customer logos | Homepage, landing pages, pricing | Choose logos your audience recognises |
| Testimonials with names and roles | Near claims and calls to action | Photos and companies increase credibility |
| Case study snippets with numbers | Landing and pricing pages | Link to full story |
| Usage numbers | Homepage, sign-up | Only if true and current |
| Third-party awards and ratings | Near the top of key pages | Link to the source |
| Guarantees and policies | Pricing, product, checkout | Free returns, money-back, cancellation terms |
| Security and compliance badges | Forms, checkout, enterprise pages | Only real certifications |
| Media mentions | Homepage | Use with care; relevance matters |
| Contact details and real people | Footer, about, checkout | Phone, address, team photos |

## When to use, and when not to
Use it when visitors hesitate at decision points, when surveys mention trust, for new brands, for high-value purchases and when entering new markets where the brand is unknown.

Don't use it to produce case studies in depth (the Product Marketing `case-studies-customer-proof` skill) or for review site strategy for AI search (the AEO & GEO `ai-citation-earning` skill).

## Inputs to ask for, in one message
1. Key pages and their conversion data.
2. Existing reviews, ratings, testimonials, case studies, logos and badges.
3. Survey or research findings about hesitation and trust.
4. Policies: returns, guarantees, cancellation, security.
5. Review collection process and platforms.
6. Markets served and any regulations on reviews and claims.

## Workflow

### Step 1. Find points of doubt
From surveys, recordings and sales notes: where do visitors hesitate and what worries them?

### Step 2. Inventory proof
List all proof by type, recency, segment and permission to use.

### Step 3. Match proof to doubts
Price worries need value proof and guarantees; quality worries need reviews and photos; security worries need certifications and policies.

### Step 4. Place proof
Near the relevant claim or action, above the fold where it matters, without cluttering.

### Step 5. Improve credibility
Names, roles, photos, dates, review counts, links to sources, verified purchase labels.

### Step 6. Fill gaps
Set up review requests after purchase or milestones, collect testimonials from happy customers, secure permissions.

### Step 7. Check compliance
No fake or incentivised reviews without disclosure; accurate claims; real badges.

### Step 8. Test
Placement and type of proof near key actions, measured on conversion.

## Review collection tips
- Ask after a positive moment (delivery, first result, renewal).
- Make leaving a review easy (one click, mobile-friendly).
- Never offer rewards for positive reviews; disclose any incentives where allowed.
- Respond to negative reviews professionally; mixed reviews can increase credibility.
- Show recent reviews and keep counts current.

## Doubt to proof map
| Visitor doubt | Proof that answers it |
|---|---|
| Will it work for someone like me? | Reviews or case studies from the same segment |
| Is it worth the price? | Results with numbers, ROI examples, guarantees |
| Is the quality good? | Photos from customers, detailed reviews, awards |
| Is this company real and safe? | Address, phone, team, security certifications |
| What if I change my mind? | Returns, cancellation and money-back terms |
| Will they support me? | Support hours, response times, named contacts |

## Credible testimonial format
```
"We cut month-end close from six days to two in our first quarter."
Jane Doe, Finance Director, Acme Distribution (120 employees)
```
A specific result, a real name, role, company and size, and ideally a photo, make a testimonial believable.

## Placement checklist
- Rating and review count next to prices and add-to-cart buttons.
- A relevant testimonial beside each key claim.
- Logos near the top of the homepage and pricing page.
- Guarantees and policies next to calls to action and in checkout.
- Security cues next to forms that ask for personal or payment data.

## Metrics
Conversion at pages where proof changed, clicks on review sections, review volume and rating trends, and survey mentions of trust concerns over time.

## Edge cases and judgement calls
| Situation | What to do |
|---|---|
| New brand with few reviews | Use founder story, guarantees, early customer quotes and transparent policies |
| B2B enterprise buyers | Security certifications, references and case studies matter most |
| Mixed ratings | Show them; respond to issues; fix root causes |
| Different markets | Use local reviews and local trust marks |
| Regulated claims | Check wording with legal; avoid unproven superlatives |

## Worked example
A meal-kit company's product pages showed a 4.6 average rating in the footer and no reviews near the subscribe button. Exit surveys mentioned doubts about portion sizes and freshness. The new design placed the rating with review count next to the price, added a review carousel filtered by household size with customer photos, showed a freshness guarantee near the button and added a line on skipping or cancelling anytime. Subscription starts from product pages rose, with the biggest lift among first-time visitors.

## Common mistakes
- Proof hidden at the bottom of pages.
- Logos irrelevant to the visitor.
- Vague testimonials with no names.
- Fake or misleading reviews and badges.
- Ignoring policies and contact details as trust signals.

## Questions to ask before you add proof
- Where do visitors hesitate, and what do they doubt?
- Which proof is most relevant to them?
- Is every claim, number and badge accurate and permitted?

## Self-check before you answer
- Is proof placed at points of doubt?
- Is it relevant, specific and credible?
- Is everything authentic and compliant?
- Are gaps filled with a collection plan?
